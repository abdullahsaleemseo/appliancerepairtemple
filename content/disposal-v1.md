## What is the disposal telling you?

| What you hear | What it usually means | What you can safely try |
|---|---|---|
| A steady hum, but nothing spins | The flywheel is jammed by something stuck between it and the grinding ring | Power off, then turn the hex socket on the bottom with an Allen wrench to free it |
| Nothing at all when you flip the switch | The motor's overload tripped, or the switch, outlet, GFCI or breaker cut power | Press the red reset button on the bottom, then check the GFCI and breaker |
| Metal grinding or rattling | Something hard is inside: a utensil, a bottle cap, a fruit pit | Power off, then look in with a flashlight and pull it out with tongs or pliers |
| It runs fine, but the sink drains slowly | The clog is past the disposal, in the trap or the drain line | Run plenty of cold water; if it stays slow, the drain needs attention |
| Loud vibration or banging | The unit has come loose from its mount, or a part inside has broken | Power off and check whether the disposal moves by hand under the sink |

## Why is my garbage disposal humming?

A hum with no spin means the motor is getting power but can't turn. Almost always, something has wedged the flywheel: a chunk of bone, a cherry pit, a sliver of glass or a shrimp tail. Left on, the motor heats up until its overload trips and it goes quiet.

To clear a jammed garbage disposal, switch it off at the wall and, if it plugs in under the sink, unplug it. Most disposals have a small hex socket in the center of the bottom. Fit an Allen wrench (many units come with one) and work it back and forth until it turns a full circle freely. Pull out whatever was stuck, using tongs. Then let the motor cool for a few minutes, press the red reset button on the bottom, restore power and run cold water before switching it on.

If it still hums once the flywheel turns freely, the disposal is not working because the motor itself is failing. On most units that points toward replacement rather than repair.

Skip chemical drain cleaners entirely. They sit in the disposal chamber, can damage the seals and metal parts, and make the job dangerous for whoever opens it up next.

## Garbage disposal leaking: top, side or bottom?

Dry the whole unit with a paper towel, fill the sink partway, then let it drain while you watch from below with a flashlight. Where the water appears tells you what's next.

At the top, where the disposal meets the sink, the sink flange seal or its plumber's putty has failed, or the mounting ring has loosened. That's usually fixable by resealing the flange.

On the side, look at the two connections: the dishwasher drain hose and the drain pipe that carries water to the trap. A loose hose clamp or a worn gasket at either one is a simple repair.

From the bottom, water is coming from inside the unit. The internal seals have worn through, and once that happens the motor isn't far behind. A disposal leaking from the bottom almost always means replacement.

## Where the dishwasher comes in

Most dishwashers drain through the disposal, so the two affect each other. A disposal packed with food waste, or a clog in its drain outlet, can leave the dishwasher with nowhere to send its water, and the dishwasher ends up holding dirty water at the end of the cycle.

A brand-new disposal brings a special case. Its dishwasher inlet comes sealed with a knockout plug, which has to be punched out from inside before the dishwasher hose goes on. If a dishwasher stops draining the day a new disposal is installed, a plug left in place is the first suspect, and [a dishwasher that won't drain](https://appliancerepairtemple.com/dishwasher-repair-temple-tx/) has a few other usual causes worth ruling out. ## Repair or replace the disposal?

Disposals are simpler than most appliances, and that cuts both ways. Jams, tripped overloads, a failed switch and loose connections are quick fixes. A worn-out motor or a failed internal seal usually isn't worth repairing, because a new unit costs about the same as rebuilding the old one, the same logic behind most [repair or replace decisions](https://appliancerepairtemple.com/blog/repair-or-replace-appliance/) on bigger appliances.

## Garbage disposal FAQ

### What should never go down a disposal?

Grease and oil, fibrous scraps like celery and corn husks, potato peels in large amounts, coffee grounds, eggshells in bulk, bones bigger than small chicken bones, pasta and rice that swell, and anything that isn't food. Small amounts with plenty of cold water are the safest habit.

### Is it safe to put ice in a disposal?

Yes, a few cubes now and then are fine. Grinding ice can knock loose small bits of buildup inside the chamber. It won't sharpen anything, though, since disposals use blunt impeller lugs rather than blades. Run cold water with it as usual.

### How long do garbage disposals last?

Many last around a decade, though it varies with the unit, how hard it works and what goes down it. A leak from the bottom, or a motor that keeps tripping its reset button, is usually the sign it's near the end of its life.

### Should I run hot or cold water with the disposal?

Cold. Cold water keeps grease solid so the disposal can chop it and send it down, instead of melting it into a film that sticks to the pipes further along. Run it before, during and for a few seconds after grinding.

### Why does my disposal smell bad?

Food bits stuck under the splash guard and in the chamber start to rot. Scrub the underside of the rubber splash guard, grind a few ice cubes with citrus peel, and run cold water for a while after each use. Skip bleach and chemical cleaners.
