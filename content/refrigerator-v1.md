## Why is my fridge not cooling but the freezer works?

Most often, cold air isn't reaching the fresh-food side from the freezer. The usual causes are an evaporator fan that has stopped, an evaporator coil buried in frost because the defrost system failed, or an air damper stuck shut between the two sections. All of them leave the freezer cold.

A technician tells these apart with the freezer's back panel off. A silent evaporator fan points to the fan motor or the board feeding it. A coil packed with ice means defrost has quit, so the heater, defrost thermostat, timer or control board gets tested with a meter. Fan running and coil clear? Then the damper is probably stuck.

Frost blockage has a tell: the fridge cools for a day or two after being unplugged, then warms up again. Mention that on a refrigerator not cooling repair call in Temple. The [refrigerator not cooling guide](https://appliancerepairtemple.com/blog/refrigerator-not-cooling/) covers the checks worth doing first.

## Refrigerator leaking water: inside the cabinet or on the floor?

Water inside the fridge, under the crisper drawers or as ice on the freezer floor, nearly always traces back to the defrost drain. Each defrost cycle melts frost off the evaporator coil, and that water runs down a line to a pan underneath. Crumbs or ice can plug the line, so the water backs up into the cabinet. A drain that keeps icing over can point to a defrost or drain heater fault.

On the floor, there are more possible sources. The drain pan can crack or get knocked out of place when the fridge is moved. On models with ice and water, the supply line, the water inlet valve and the filter housing are all places a slow drip starts. Filter heads also crack when a filter is twisted in too hard.

To narrow it down, dry the floor, tuck a paper towel under the front edge and another at the back, and check them in an hour. The [refrigerator leaking water guide](https://appliancerepairtemple.com/blog/refrigerator-leaking-water/) goes further.

## Refrigerator making loud noise: which sounds are normal?

**Normal.** A low hum while the compressor runs, gurgling as refrigerant moves, a click at start and stop, cubes dropping and a sizzle during defrost.

**Ticking or scraping inside the freezer.** Usually the evaporator fan blade catching on ice. That's a defrost problem showing up early.

**Rattling at the bottom back.** Often the condenser fan, clogged with lint and pet hair until it wobbles, or a loose drain pan.

**Click, quiet, click again with no cooling.** The compressor is trying to start and the overload keeps shutting it off. Often that's the start relay; sometimes it's the compressor, and only testing tells which.

Ice maker repair in Temple has a local twist: much of the area's water is hard. ## How much is refrigerator repair?

Mostly it depends on which part failed. A start relay, a defrost thermostat or a damper is a small part and a short job. An evaporator fan or defrost heater takes longer, since the freezer has to be emptied and its back panel removed. Electronic boards cost more as parts. At the far end, a compressor or refrigerant leak means brazing, pulling a vacuum and recharging, a much bigger job.

Installation matters too: a built-in or boxed-in fridge adds labor, and discontinued models can wait on shipped parts.

## Fridge repair or replace: where's the line?

Standard refrigerators last roughly 10 to 15 years, and age gives a quick first answer. Under about seven years, nearly any repair short of the sealed system is worth doing. Between seven and twelve, it depends on the part. Past twelve, stick to the inexpensive fixes.

**Usually worth fixing at any age:** door gaskets, a start relay, a defrost heater or thermostat, a fan motor, a clogged drain or a water inlet valve.

**Often not worth it on an older fridge:** a refrigerant leak inside the foam walls, a failed board on a discontinued model, a cracked inner liner or a second sealed system repair.

Plain opinion from the bench: past twelve years, a sealed system repair rarely pays off.

Garage fridges are their own case: most are built for indoor temperatures, so if one fails every summer in a Temple garage, the location is the real problem.

## Refrigerator repair FAQ

### How long should a refrigerator take to cool after a repair?

Plan on several hours. The fresh-food side usually gets back to 40°F or below within the day, and the freezer can take up to 24 hours to reach 0°F. Keep the doors closed and load food back gradually, because a full load of warm groceries slows the pull-down.

### Is it normal for the side of my fridge to feel hot?

Yes, usually. Many refrigerators run condenser tubing through the side walls or around the door opening to stop condensation. Warm to the touch is normal. Too hot to keep your hand on, together with poor cooling, is worth a call, since it can point to an airflow or condenser problem.

### Can I clean the condenser coils myself?

Usually, yes. Unplug the fridge, find the coils behind the bottom grille or on the back, and use a coil brush and a vacuum. Once or twice a year is a good habit, more often with pets. If the coils sit in a sealed compartment you can't reach, leave them for a technician.

### Why is my refrigerator freezing food in the fresh-food section?

Most often the temperature is set too cold or food is blocking the vent where freezer air comes in. If neither is the case, a damper stuck open or a failed temperature sensor lets in too much cold air. A technician tests the sensor and watches whether the damper opens and closes.

### Should I replace the water filter before calling about slow water or ice?

Yes, if it's due or you don't know when it was last changed. An overdue filter is the easiest fix for slow flow, and a fresh one rules it out. Use a filter made for your model, since a poor fit can leak at the filter housing.
